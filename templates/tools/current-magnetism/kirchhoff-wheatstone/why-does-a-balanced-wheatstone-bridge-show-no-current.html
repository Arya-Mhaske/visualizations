{% extends "tools/base_tool.html" %} {% block lead %}A bridge is two potential dividers side by side. Here height is potential, so B and D sit at their true levels. Drag D until the bridge wire lies flat.{% endblock %} {% block why_text %}Current in a wire needs a potential difference between its ends. Each side of the bridge splits the battery's voltage in the ratio of its resistors. If both sides split it in the same ratio, R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄, then B and D are at the same potential. Nothing pushes charge across, so no current flows, whatever the battery.{% endblock %} {% block try_items %}
  • Drag D until the galvanometer reads zero. Compare R₁/R₂ with R₃/R₄.
  • Now change the battery and the galvanometer. Does the balance survive?
  • At balance, cut the bridge wire. Do B and D move? Try again away from balance.
  • Load the NCERT Example and find the galvanometer current. Which R₄ would balance it?
    • {% endblock %} {% block notes %}
    Note This is why a bridge measures an unknown resistance so well. You look for a zero, so the meter does not need a true scale, and the result does not depend on the battery. The needle here is not to scale: small currents are stretched so you can see them.
