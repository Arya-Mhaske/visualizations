{% extends "tools/base_tool.html" %} {% block lead %}Cut a bar magnet in two. Then cut again. Do you ever end up with a north pole on its own?{% endblock %} {% block why_text %}A magnet's field comes from tiny current loops, and every loop has two faces, a north and a south. Cutting the bar just separates the loops into groups. Each piece still has its loops pointing the same way, so each piece still has a north end and a south end. A lone pole would need a loop with only one face.{% endblock %} {% block try_items %}
  • Cut once, then compare the two pieces with the original. What do you see?
  • Keep cutting. Count the north poles and the south poles. Do the counts ever differ?
  • Look at the arrows below the bar. Do the cuts change which way they point?
    • {% endblock %} {% block notes %}
    Note Physicists have searched for a single magnetic pole, called a monopole. None has been found. Down at one atom the pattern is the same: the atom is a tiny loop of current, or a spinning charge, and it has two poles.
